# 1. POLITY AND GOVERNANCE

[↑ Back to Monthly Index](../CURRENT_AFFAIRS_JULY_2026.md#toc)

July 2026 was a defining month for India's institutions. The Election Commission's Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls — accompanied by the deletion of lakhs of names in Odisha, fresh attestation forms and an unprecedented internal dispute within the Commission — brought questions of citizenship, franchise and institutional neutrality to the fore. Parliament, meanwhile, continued its extraordinary turn, taking up the anti-paper leak law (see Chapter 9) and a Bill to expand the Supreme Court from 34 to 38 judges amid record-low productivity. The main items and the news-in-shorts below capture the polity, governance and legal developments that mattered in the month.

---

<a id="sec-1-1"></a>
## 1.1. SPECIAL INTENSIVE REVISION (SIR) 2026: PURGING OR PRUNING OF ELECTORAL ROLLS?

**Why in News?**
- The **Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls** ordered by the **Election Commission of India (ECI)**, which began through June–July 2026, became the biggest governance controversy of the month.
- On **5 July 2026**, the ECI announced that **over 20 lakh names had been removed** in the **first phase of the SIR in Odisha**, inviting charges of "mass disenfranchisement", especially of minority communities, Dalits and migrant workers.
- On **17 July 2026**, a three-judge Supreme Court Bench ruled that **removal from the electoral roll does not imply loss of citizenship**, and that **the ECI cannot adjudicate citizenship** — a power that rests exclusively with the competent authority under the Citizenship Act, 1955.
- The exercise was further complicated by an **internal dispute within the ECI**, in which two Election Commissioners objected to certain decisions, culminating in a **28 July 2026 work-allocation order** by the Chief Election Commissioner.

**About Special Intensive Revision of electoral rolls**
- The electoral rolls of every constituency are revised under the **Representation of the People Act, 1950** (Section 21) — a power the ECI exercises under **Article 324** of the Constitution — and the *Registration of Electors Rules, 1960*.
- Revision is of three broad types: **summary** (carried out each year), **intensive** (more detailed door-to-door verification) and **special intensive revision (SIR)**, where rolls are re-verified book-wise with special focus on deleting duplicate, dead and shifted entries and adding new electors.
- The 2026 SIR uses **digitised, Aadhaar-enabled** forms: **Form 6** (new registration), **Form 6A** (overseas elector), **Form 7** (objection to inclusion / claim for deletion) and **Form 8/8A** (corrections). A key point of controversy was a **modified Form 6** (from **12 July 2026**) incorporating a new **SIR Declaration** and requiring details of parents/guardians for fresh applicants.
- The ECI justified the drive as a pre-election "roll cleansing" exercise before the **2027 Assembly elections** in several States and ahead of possible simultaneous elections.

| Type of Revision | Frequency | Method | Remarks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Summary revision | Annual | Roll-to-roll verification | `ss. 21(1)-(2A)`, R.P. Act 1950 |
| Intensive revision | Periodic as ordered | Door-to-door enumeration | Detailed verification of each household, `s. 21(3)` |
| Special Intensive Revision (SIR) | As ordered (2026) | Phased, book-wise, Aadhaar-linked | Deletions + fresh inclusions; most contentious |

**Key developments in July 2026**
- **Odisha deletions:** The first phase of the State's SIR struck off more than 20 lakh names, mostly as duplicate/deceased/adversely-adjudged entries; thousands of others were added. The move was challenged in the High Court and the Supreme Court.
- **Supreme Court judgment (17 July 2026):** The Court clarified that entry in, or removal from, an electoral roll is not proof of citizenship — only the Centre or a competent authority under the **Citizenship Act, 1955** can determine citizenship. It also instructed that owners of deleted entries be given a hearing and that the statutory revisory procedure under `ss. 22–23` of the R.P. Act, 1950 be strictly followed.
- **Delhi timeline:** Following petitions by students and civil-society groups, the **ECI extended the Delhi SIR schedule**, with the final electoral roll set for publication on **19 October 2026**.
- **ECI-internal rift:** Two **Election Commissioners** publicly recorded objections to the way the SIR was being executed, and the **28 July work-allocation order** redistributed functions within the Commission. The **Congress and other parties** demanded an inquiry and an end to the exercise.
- **Opposition to the CJI:** In late July, leaders of **23 Opposition parties wrote to the Chief Justice of India**, seeking urgent judicial scrutiny of the SIR and alleging that the deletions and new forms were designed to "purge" the rolls before elections.

**Concerns and criticism**
- **Citizenship vs franchise:** Using roll-deletion as a proxy for citizenship adjudication conflates two separate legal regimes and risks denying a citizen's right to vote under Article 326.
- **Procedural fairness:** Mass deletions without individual notices and hearings violate the principles of natural justice embedded in `ss. 22–23` of the R.P. Act, 1950.
- **Data and privacy:** Mandatory Aadhaar-linked forms and the new parent-detail requirement raise privacy concerns under the DPDP Act, 2023 and the Aadhaar Act, 2016.
- **Institutional neutrality:** The internal differences within the ECI have been seen as eroding the credibility of the constitutional body charged with free and fair elections.

**Way Forward**
- Adhere strictly to the statutory revisory procedure, issue person-wise notices, and provide an effective appeal/re-enrolment route (Form 6) for affected citizens.
- Complete the exercise against a **transparent, biometric-free differential** between legitimate deletion (death, shift, duplicate) and controversial categories, with periodic publication of deletion data.
- Resolve procedural differences within the ECI through a clear, published code, and recall that the electoral roll is a means of realising democracy, not a citizenship test.

*Source: PIB (ECI); The Hindu, 6 & 17 Jul 2026; Live Law, 17 Jul 2026; The Indian Express, 12 Jul 2026; The Wire, Late-Jul 2026*

## 1.2. SUPREME COURT (NUMBER OF JUDGES) AMENDMENT BILL, 2026

**Why in News?**
- The **Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Bill, 2026**, seeking to raise the sanctioned strength of the Supreme Court from **34 to 38 judges** (including the Chief Justice of India), was **introduced in the Lok Sabha on 20 July 2026**.
- The Bill replaces the **Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Ordinance, 2026** promulgated in **May 2026**, which had already raised the strength; Parliament was required to convert the Ordinance into legislation within six weeks of reassembly.
- (It was passed by the Lok Sabha on 3 August and the Rajya Sabha on 5 August 2026, and received presidential assent in August 2026.)

**About the measure**
- Under **Article 124(1)** of the Constitution, the strength of the Supreme Court is not fixed by the Constitution itself; **Parliament may by law regulate** its number of judges.
- The sanctioned strength has been raised over time — from 8 judges at inception (1950) to 18 (1977), 26 (1986), 31 (2009) and 34 (2019, including the CJI).
- The 2026 change is pushed on the ground of **expanding pendency**: over **92,000 cases** were pending before the Supreme Court, including long-pending admission matters and a growing number of Constitution Bench references.
- It also follows a push from the judiciary itself — **Chief Justice Surya Kant's letter** to the President and the Prime Minister pointed to the Court's workload and the need for more permanent judges and benches.
- **Appointment process:** Judges are appointed by the President on the recommendation of the **Collegium** (CJI + four senior-most judges) under Article 124(2). The alternative — the **National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC)** created by the 99th Constitutional Amendment (2014) — was struck down by the Supreme Court in *Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015)* as violative of judicial independence.

| Feature | Details |
|---|---|
| Bill | Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Bill, 2026 |
| Change | Sanctioned strength raised 34 → 38 (incl. CJI) |
| Legal basis | Art. 124(1); replaces Ordinance promulgated May 2026 |
| Timeline | Introduced LS 20 Jul 2026; passed LS 3 Aug, RS 5 Aug 2026 |
| Driver | Case pendency >92,000; CJI's letter; need for more permanent benches |

**Controversy: the Money Bill route**
- The Bill was introduced with the **Speaker's certification as a Money Bill** under **Article 110**, allowing it to be passed by the Lok Sabha alone and to bypass Rajya Sabha scrutiny.
- Critics argued that a measure affecting the composition of a constitutional court is not a Money Bill and that certification has been stretched beyond its constitutional purpose — a concern also raised in the context of other recent legislation.
- Supporters countered that the increase entails additional expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund of India, which in their reading brings it within Article 110.

**Significance**
- Adding judges does not by itself fix the adversarial bottlenecks of the apex court — the strengthening of **Constitution Benches and the admission/appeal grid** is equally vital.
- The episode highlights the recurring tension between the **executive (Parliament's power over court size and the Money Bill route) and the judiciary (Collegium-driven appointments)** — a classic prelims-relevant institutional theme.
- For UPSC aspirants, the case is best remembered as: **the 2026 Bill raises SC strength to 38 under Article 124(1), replaces a May 2026 Ordinance, and was passed amid a Money Bill controversy.**

*Source: PRS India; News On AIR & The Hindu, 20 Jul 2026; Lok Sabha Secretariat, Aug 2026*

## 1.3. NEWS IN SHORTS

<a id="sec-1-3-1"></a>
### 1.3.1. 'VANDE MATARAM' GETS STATUTORY PROTECTION (PREVENTION OF INSULTS TO NATIONAL HONOUR AMENDMENT)
- The **Rajya Sabha passed** the Prevention of Insults to National Honour (Amendment) Bill, 2026 on **29 July 2026** and the **Lok Sabha passed it on 30 July 2026**.
- The Bill adds **"Vande Mataram"** to the categories of national symbols whose **insult is punishable with up to three years' imprisonment and fine** — the same protection already available for the National Flag, the State Emblem and the National Anthem under the Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971.
- Composed by **Bankim Chandra Chatterjee** in the novel *Anandamath* (1882), "Vande Mataram" was a rallying cry of the freedom movement and the **first two stanzas are sung on national occasions**; under **Article 51A(a)**, every citizen owes respect to the National Flag, the National Anthem and the Constitution.
- The move also raises the question of the **"national song"** — the Constitution names neither a national song nor distinguishes it from the national anthem — and linking the song's protection to the 1971 Act is Parliament's way of remedying that legal gap.
- *Source: Rajya Sabha & Lok Sabha, 29–30 Jul 2026*

<a id="sec-1-3-2"></a>
### 1.3.2. ONE NATION, ONE ELECTION: CONSULTATIONS, ECI READINESS AND COMMITTEE EXTENSION
- The **Joint Committee of Parliament on the Constitution (One Nation, One Election) Bills** held State-level consultations through July: it **concluded its Goa consultations on 11 July 2026** and held further sittings in Lucknow/Uttar Pradesh on **14–15 July 2026** with MLAs, academics and officials.
- The **ECI told the panel on 15 July 2026** that it is prepared to implement simultaneous elections once a **six-month notice** is given.
- On **29 July 2026**, the Lok Sabha **extended the tenure of the committee till the Winter Session** of Parliament to submit its report.
- The ONOE reform envisages simultaneous elections to the Lok Sabha, State Assemblies and (in the second phase) local bodies, resting on **Articles 83 and 172** (duration of Houses), a proposed **"appointed date"** for mid-term polls, and **common electoral rolls** with the ECI.
- *Source: PIB, 11 Jul 2026; News On AIR, The Hindu & Economic Times, 14–29 Jul 2026*

<a id="sec-1-3-3"></a>
### 1.3.3. GYANVAPI, MATHURA (SHAHI IDGAH) AND SAMBHAL DISPUTES REFERRED TO SPECIAL LOK ADALAT
- On **13 July 2026**, a bench led by the **Chief Justice of India** referred the **Gyanvapi (Varanasi), Shri Krishna Janmabhoomi–Shahi Idgah (Mathura) and Sambhal mosque** land disputes to a **Special Lok Adalat** for amicable settlement.
- On **13–14 July**, however, **the Hindu and Muslim litigating sides both rejected** the mediation route, insisting on adjudication of their claims.
- The Court's push for settlement draws on **Section 89 of the CPC** (compromise through lok adalats/mediation) and the idea of **alternative dispute resolution** to ease the pendency of high-stakes property-cum-religion cases; earlier (5 July 2026) a lok adalat had been held in Mathura after Supreme Court directions.
- Related to the **Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act, 1991** disputes, the case anchors the debate on **court-supervised mediation versus adjudication in sensitive inter-religious property suits**.
- *Source: The Hindu, LawBeat & The Wire, 13–14 Jul 2026*

<a id="sec-1-3-4"></a>
### 1.3.4. SUPREME COURT'S SELF-CORRECTION: REVIEW OF VACATION INTERIM ORDERS
- On **3 July 2026**, the Supreme Court took the unusual step of **reviewing about 450 interim/ex-parte orders and observations** made during the six-week summer vacation, announcing that such orders issued without adversarial hearing would be **taken up for 'open court' hearing**.
- The move followed criticism that vacation-bench orders had occasionally contained **unsolicited, binding observations** and directions that could not be orally varied later.
- The Chief Justice observed that interim orders ought not to become final in effect, and that the judges should avoid **"gratuitous remarks"** while passing ex-parte reliefs.
- The development is significant for the debate on **judicial discipline, transparency and the finality of interim orders** in a right-to-fair-trial framework.
- *Source: The Hindu; Economic Times; Bar & Bench, 3 Jul 2026*

<a id="sec-1-3-5"></a>
### 1.3.5. APAAR IDs: SUPREME COURT ORDERS PAN-INDIA 'OPT-OUT' RULE
- On **20–21 July 2026**, the Supreme Court directed the **CBSE and the Union Government to implement the Orissa High Court's ruling on APAAR ID consent forms across the country**, holding that **parents cannot be forced to create APAAR IDs** for their children.
- **APAAR (Automated Permanent Academic Account Registry)** is the "One Student, One ID" digital education registry under the **National Education Policy 2020**, linked to the DigiLocker and Aadhaar ecosystem through UDISE+.
- The Court directed that the consent form must carry an **explicit opt-out option**, that student data shall not be shared beyond lawful purposes, and that **refusal** of consent must not be treated as denial of education or examination.
- The ruling extends the DPDP Act, 2023's **consent-and-purpose-limitation** principles to the education sector and restores parental choice in the face of mandatory data collection.
- *Source: The Hindu; SCC Online; Deccan Herald, 20–21 Jul 2026*

<a id="sec-1-3-6"></a>
### 1.3.6. NEET-UG 2026: SUPREME COURT ON THE RIGHT TO PEACEFUL PROTEST
- Amid nationwide protests over the **NEET-UG 2026 paper leak** and the police crackdown of **20 July 2026** on the "Sansad Chalo" march, the **Supreme Court heard habeas corpus and plea matters on 27–28 July 2026**.
- The CJI-led bench upheld the **right to peaceful agitate as part of freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a)**, declaring that *"there should be no restrictions on the right to agitate"*, while restraining disproportionate state action.
- The Court **ordered the release of minor students** from custody, stayed coercive action against student bodies, and required the police to furnish details of arrests.
- On **30 July 2026**, the **Delhi Government withdrew the legal action against the student umbrella body CJP** in deference to the Supreme Court's observations.
- The sequence is a model prelims case on the **constitutional hierarchy of rights, the duties of the police in public protest, and the circumstances under which criminal law discipline must yield to democratic participation**.
- *Source: Live Law; The Hindu; Indian Express, 27–30 Jul 2026*

<a id="sec-1-3-7"></a>
### 1.3.7. SUPREME COURT URGES STANDALONE OFFENCE FOR 'DIGITAL ARRESTS'
- On **28 July 2026**, while hearing petitions on the menace of **'digital arrest' scams**, the Supreme Court **called for a separate, clearly defined offence and harsher punishment** for such frauds.
- Digital arrest scams involve fraudsters posing as police/CBI/customs officials over video calls, "arresting" victims online and coercing money transfers; losses of over ₹54,000 crore have been flagged to the Court.
- The bench pressed the RBI, banks and telecom companies to escalate their response. (Full pan-India directions, including RBI's SOP on **"mule accounts"**, followed in early August 2026.)
- The case blends **cyber-crime policing, consumer/regulatory law and the constitutionality of banking compensation** — a recurring UPSC theme.
- *Source: Bar & Bench; Live Law, 28 Jul 2026*

<a id="sec-1-3-8"></a>
### 1.3.8. CENTRE WEIGHS CBFC CERTIFICATION FOR OTT FILMS AFTER 'SATLUJ' ROW
- In mid-July 2026, following the **takedown of the film 'Satluj'** and the enforcement action against streaming platform ZEE5 over the release of the politically sensitive documentary, the **Information & Broadcasting Ministry indicated it is examining CBFC certification for OTT films**.
- Theatrical releases are pre-censored under the **Cinematograph Act, 1952**, while OTT content is self-regulated under the **IT (Intermediary Guidelines & Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021**'s three-tier mechanism.
- Bringing streaming films under CBFC would represent a major shift towards **uniform content regulation**, raising questions of pre-censorship and the constitutionality of content rules in the digital age.
- *Source: The Indian Express; The New Indian Express, 10–15 Jul 2026*

<a id="sec-1-3-9"></a>
### 1.3.9. PASSPORT FEES HIKED FROM 1 JULY 2026
- From **1 July 2026**, the cost of a fresh Indian passport for a standard 36-page booklet rose to **₹2,500**, and the Tatkal fee to **₹5,000**, following revised tariffs notified by the Ministry of External Affairs at the end of June 2026.
- Fees vary with the **number of pages** (36/64) and the mode (normal/Tatkal), and include consular and printing charges.
- The hike, the first substantial revision in years, is explained by **modernisation of the Passport Seva Kendras** and secure booklet production; over **1.5 crore** passports are issued annually.
- *Source: The Times of India; Ministry of External Affairs, 1 Jul 2026*

<a id="sec-1-3-10"></a>
### 1.3.10. NEET-UG 2026: CBI'S ~20,000-PAGE CHARGE-SHEET
- On **29 July 2026**, the **CBI filed a chargesheet of about 20,000 pages** before a **fast-track court** in connection with the NEET-UG 2026 paper leak, naming examinees, middlemen and accused institutions.
- The probe, transferred to the CBI after the leak was detected in May 2026, is being fast-tracked under the provisions of the **Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act** and the newly passed 2026 amendment (see Chapter 9).
- The chargesheet is expected to be followed by the framing of charges and trial within the timelines envisioned by the amended anti-paper-leak law, which mandates **completion of investigation in about two months** and **disposal of trial in a fast-track court**.
- *Source: The Hindu; PTI, 29 Jul 2026*
