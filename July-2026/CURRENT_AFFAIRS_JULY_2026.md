# 1. POLITY AND GOVERNANCE

July 2026 was a defining month for India's institutions. The Election Commission's Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls — accompanied by the deletion of lakhs of names in Odisha, fresh attestation forms and an unprecedented internal dispute within the Commission — brought questions of citizenship, franchise and institutional neutrality to the fore. Parliament, meanwhile, continued its extraordinary turn, taking up the anti-paper leak law (see Chapter 9) and a Bill to expand the Supreme Court from 34 to 38 judges amid record-low productivity. The main items and the news-in-shorts below capture the polity, governance and legal developments that mattered in the month.

## 1.1. SPECIAL INTENSIVE REVISION (SIR) 2026: PURGING OR PRUNING OF ELECTORAL ROLLS?

**Why in News?**
- The **Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls** ordered by the **Election Commission of India (ECI)**, which began through June–July 2026, became the biggest governance controversy of the month.
- On **5 July 2026**, the ECI announced that **over 20 lakh names had been removed** in the **first phase of the SIR in Odisha**, inviting charges of "mass disenfranchisement", especially of minority communities, Dalits and migrant workers.
- On **17 July 2026**, a three-judge Supreme Court Bench ruled that **removal from the electoral roll does not imply loss of citizenship**, and that **the ECI cannot adjudicate citizenship** — a power that rests exclusively with the competent authority under the Citizenship Act, 1955.
- The exercise was further complicated by an **internal dispute within the ECI**, in which two Election Commissioners objected to certain decisions, culminating in a **28 July 2026 work-allocation order** by the Chief Election Commissioner.

**About Special Intensive Revision of electoral rolls**
- The electoral rolls of every constituency are revised under the **Representation of the People Act, 1950** (Section 21) — a power the ECI exercises under **Article 324** of the Constitution — and the *Registration of Electors Rules, 1960*.
- Revision is of three broad types: **summary** (carried out each year), **intensive** (more detailed door-to-door verification) and **special intensive revision (SIR)**, where rolls are re-verified book-wise with special focus on deleting duplicate, dead and shifted entries and adding new electors.
- The 2026 SIR uses **digitised, Aadhaar-enabled** forms: **Form 6** (new registration), **Form 6A** (overseas elector), **Form 7** (objection to inclusion / claim for deletion) and **Form 8/8A** (corrections). A key point of controversy was a **modified Form 6** (from **12 July 2026**) incorporating a new **SIR Declaration** and requiring details of parents/guardians for fresh applicants.
- The ECI justified the drive as a pre-election "roll cleansing" exercise before the **2027 Assembly elections** in several States and ahead of possible simultaneous elections.

| Type of Revision | Frequency | Method | Remarks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Summary revision | Annual | Roll-to-roll verification | `ss. 21(1)-(2A)`, R.P. Act 1950 |
| Intensive revision | Periodic as ordered | Door-to-door enumeration | Detailed verification of each household, `s. 21(3)` |
| Special Intensive Revision (SIR) | As ordered (2026) | Phased, book-wise, Aadhaar-linked | Deletions + fresh inclusions; most contentious |

**Key developments in July 2026**
- **Odisha deletions:** The first phase of the State's SIR struck off more than 20 lakh names, mostly as duplicate/deceased/adversely-adjudged entries; thousands of others were added. The move was challenged in the High Court and the Supreme Court.
- **Supreme Court judgment (17 July 2026):** The Court clarified that entry in, or removal from, an electoral roll is not proof of citizenship — only the Centre or a competent authority under the **Citizenship Act, 1955** can determine citizenship. It also instructed that owners of deleted entries be given a hearing and that the statutory revisory procedure under `ss. 22–23` of the R.P. Act, 1950 be strictly followed.
- **Delhi timeline:** Following petitions by students and civil-society groups, the **ECI extended the Delhi SIR schedule**, with the final electoral roll set for publication on **19 October 2026**.
- **ECI-internal rift:** Two **Election Commissioners** publicly recorded objections to the way the SIR was being executed, and the **28 July work-allocation order** redistributed functions within the Commission. The **Congress and other parties** demanded an inquiry and an end to the exercise.
- **Opposition to the CJI:** In late July, leaders of **23 Opposition parties wrote to the Chief Justice of India**, seeking urgent judicial scrutiny of the SIR and alleging that the deletions and new forms were designed to "purge" the rolls before elections.

**Concerns and criticism**
- **Citizenship vs franchise:** Using roll-deletion as a proxy for citizenship adjudication conflates two separate legal regimes and risks denying a citizen's right to vote under Article 326.
- **Procedural fairness:** Mass deletions without individual notices and hearings violate the principles of natural justice embedded in `ss. 22–23` of the R.P. Act, 1950.
- **Data and privacy:** Mandatory Aadhaar-linked forms and the new parent-detail requirement raise privacy concerns under the DPDP Act, 2023 and the Aadhaar Act, 2016.
- **Institutional neutrality:** The internal differences within the ECI have been seen as eroding the credibility of the constitutional body charged with free and fair elections.

**Way Forward**
- Adhere strictly to the statutory revisory procedure, issue person-wise notices, and provide an effective appeal/re-enrolment route (Form 6) for affected citizens.
- Complete the exercise against a **transparent, biometric-free differential** between legitimate deletion (death, shift, duplicate) and controversial categories, with periodic publication of deletion data.
- Resolve procedural differences within the ECI through a clear, published code, and recall that the electoral roll is a means of realising democracy, not a citizenship test.

*Source: PIB (ECI); The Hindu, 6 & 17 Jul 2026; Live Law, 17 Jul 2026; The Indian Express, 12 Jul 2026; The Wire, Late-Jul 2026*

## 1.2. SUPREME COURT (NUMBER OF JUDGES) AMENDMENT BILL, 2026

**Why in News?**
- The **Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Bill, 2026**, seeking to raise the sanctioned strength of the Supreme Court from **34 to 38 judges** (including the Chief Justice of India), was **introduced in the Lok Sabha on 20 July 2026**.
- The Bill replaces the **Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Ordinance, 2026** promulgated in **May 2026**, which had already raised the strength; Parliament was required to convert the Ordinance into legislation within six weeks of reassembly.
- (It was passed by the Lok Sabha on 3 August and the Rajya Sabha on 5 August 2026, and received presidential assent in August 2026.)

**About the measure**
- Under **Article 124(1)** of the Constitution, the strength of the Supreme Court is not fixed by the Constitution itself; **Parliament may by law regulate** its number of judges.
- The sanctioned strength has been raised over time — from 8 judges at inception (1950) to 18 (1977), 26 (1986), 31 (2009) and 34 (2019, including the CJI).
- The 2026 change is pushed on the ground of **expanding pendency**: over **92,000 cases** were pending before the Supreme Court, including long-pending admission matters and a growing number of Constitution Bench references.
- It also follows a push from the judiciary itself — **Chief Justice Surya Kant's letter** to the President and the Prime Minister pointed to the Court's workload and the need for more permanent judges and benches.
- **Appointment process:** Judges are appointed by the President on the recommendation of the **Collegium** (CJI + four senior-most judges) under Article 124(2). The alternative — the **National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC)** created by the 99th Constitutional Amendment (2014) — was struck down by the Supreme Court in *Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015)* as violative of judicial independence.

| Feature | Details |
|---|---|
| Bill | Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Bill, 2026 |
| Change | Sanctioned strength raised 34 → 38 (incl. CJI) |
| Legal basis | Art. 124(1); replaces Ordinance promulgated May 2026 |
| Timeline | Introduced LS 20 Jul 2026; passed LS 3 Aug, RS 5 Aug 2026 |
| Driver | Case pendency >92,000; CJI's letter; need for more permanent benches |

**Controversy: the Money Bill route**
- The Bill was introduced with the **Speaker's certification as a Money Bill** under **Article 110**, allowing it to be passed by the Lok Sabha alone and to bypass Rajya Sabha scrutiny.
- Critics argued that a measure affecting the composition of a constitutional court is not a Money Bill and that certification has been stretched beyond its constitutional purpose — a concern also raised in the context of other recent legislation.
- Supporters countered that the increase entails additional expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund of India, which in their reading brings it within Article 110.

**Significance**
- Adding judges does not by itself fix the adversarial bottlenecks of the apex court — the strengthening of **Constitution Benches and the admission/appeal grid** is equally vital.
- The episode highlights the recurring tension between the **executive (Parliament's power over court size and the Money Bill route) and the judiciary (Collegium-driven appointments)** — a classic prelims-relevant institutional theme.
- For UPSC aspirants, the case is best remembered as: **the 2026 Bill raises SC strength to 38 under Article 124(1), replaces a May 2026 Ordinance, and was passed amid a Money Bill controversy.**

*Source: PRS India; News On AIR & The Hindu, 20 Jul 2026; Lok Sabha Secretariat, Aug 2026*

## 1.3. NEWS IN SHORTS

### 1.3.1. 'VANDE MATARAM' GETS STATUTORY PROTECTION (PREVENTION OF INSULTS TO NATIONAL HONOUR AMENDMENT)
- The **Rajya Sabha passed** the Prevention of Insults to National Honour (Amendment) Bill, 2026 on **29 July 2026** and the **Lok Sabha passed it on 30 July 2026**.
- The Bill adds **"Vande Mataram"** to the categories of national symbols whose **insult is punishable with up to three years' imprisonment and fine** — the same protection already available for the National Flag, the State Emblem and the National Anthem under the Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971.
- Composed by **Bankim Chandra Chatterjee** in the novel *Anandamath* (1882), "Vande Mataram" was a rallying cry of the freedom movement and the **first two stanzas are sung on national occasions**; under **Article 51A(a)**, every citizen owes respect to the National Flag, the National Anthem and the Constitution.
- The move also raises the question of the **"national song"** — the Constitution names neither a national song nor distinguishes it from the national anthem — and linking the song's protection to the 1971 Act is Parliament's way of remedying that legal gap.
- *Source: Rajya Sabha & Lok Sabha, 29–30 Jul 2026*

### 1.3.2. ONE NATION, ONE ELECTION: CONSULTATIONS, ECI READINESS AND COMMITTEE EXTENSION
- The **Joint Committee of Parliament on the Constitution (One Nation, One Election) Bills** held State-level consultations through July: it **concluded its Goa consultations on 11 July 2026** and held further sittings in Lucknow/Uttar Pradesh on **14–15 July 2026** with MLAs, academics and officials.
- The **ECI told the panel on 15 July 2026** that it is prepared to implement simultaneous elections once a **six-month notice** is given.
- On **29 July 2026**, the Lok Sabha **extended the tenure of the committee till the Winter Session** of Parliament to submit its report.
- The ONOE reform envisages simultaneous elections to the Lok Sabha, State Assemblies and (in the second phase) local bodies, resting on **Articles 83 and 172** (duration of Houses), a proposed **"appointed date"** for mid-term polls, and **common electoral rolls** with the ECI.
- *Source: PIB, 11 Jul 2026; News On AIR, The Hindu & Economic Times, 14–29 Jul 2026*

### 1.3.3. GYANVAPI, MATHURA (SHAHI IDGAH) AND SAMBHAL DISPUTES REFERRED TO SPECIAL LOK ADALAT
- On **13 July 2026**, a bench led by the **Chief Justice of India** referred the **Gyanvapi (Varanasi), Shri Krishna Janmabhoomi–Shahi Idgah (Mathura) and Sambhal mosque** land disputes to a **Special Lok Adalat** for amicable settlement.
- On **13–14 July**, however, **the Hindu and Muslim litigating sides both rejected** the mediation route, insisting on adjudication of their claims.
- The Court's push for settlement draws on **Section 89 of the CPC** (compromise through lok adalats/mediation) and the idea of **alternative dispute resolution** to ease the pendency of high-stakes property-cum-religion cases; earlier (5 July 2026) a lok adalat had been held in Mathura after Supreme Court directions.
- Related to the **Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act, 1991** disputes, the case anchors the debate on **court-supervised mediation versus adjudication in sensitive inter-religious property suits**.
- *Source: The Hindu, LawBeat & The Wire, 13–14 Jul 2026*

### 1.3.4. SUPREME COURT'S SELF-CORRECTION: REVIEW OF VACATION INTERIM ORDERS
- On **3 July 2026**, the Supreme Court took the unusual step of **reviewing about 450 interim/ex-parte orders and observations** made during the six-week summer vacation, announcing that such orders issued without adversarial hearing would be **taken up for 'open court' hearing**.
- The move followed criticism that vacation-bench orders had occasionally contained **unsolicited, binding observations** and directions that could not be orally varied later.
- The Chief Justice observed that interim orders ought not to become final in effect, and that the judges should avoid **"gratuitous remarks"** while passing ex-parte reliefs.
- The development is significant for the debate on **judicial discipline, transparency and the finality of interim orders** in a right-to-fair-trial framework.
- *Source: The Hindu; Economic Times; Bar & Bench, 3 Jul 2026*

### 1.3.5. APAAR IDs: SUPREME COURT ORDERS PAN-INDIA 'OPT-OUT' RULE
- On **20–21 July 2026**, the Supreme Court directed the **CBSE and the Union Government to implement the Orissa High Court's ruling on APAAR ID consent forms across the country**, holding that **parents cannot be forced to create APAAR IDs** for their children.
- **APAAR (Automated Permanent Academic Account Registry)** is the "One Student, One ID" digital education registry under the **National Education Policy 2020**, linked to the DigiLocker and Aadhaar ecosystem through UDISE+.
- The Court directed that the consent form must carry an **explicit opt-out option**, that student data shall not be shared beyond lawful purposes, and that **refusal** of consent must not be treated as denial of education or examination.
- The ruling extends the DPDP Act, 2023's **consent-and-purpose-limitation** principles to the education sector and restores parental choice in the face of mandatory data collection.
- *Source: The Hindu; SCC Online; Deccan Herald, 20–21 Jul 2026*

### 1.3.6. NEET-UG 2026: SUPREME COURT ON THE RIGHT TO PEACEFUL PROTEST
- Amid nationwide protests over the **NEET-UG 2026 paper leak** and the police crackdown of **20 July 2026** on the "Sansad Chalo" march, the **Supreme Court heard habeas corpus and plea matters on 27–28 July 2026**.
- The CJI-led bench upheld the **right to peaceful agitate as part of freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a)**, declaring that *"there should be no restrictions on the right to agitate"*, while restraining disproportionate state action.
- The Court **ordered the release of minor students** from custody, stayed coercive action against student bodies, and required the police to furnish details of arrests.
- On **30 July 2026**, the **Delhi Government withdrew the legal action against the student umbrella body CJP** in deference to the Supreme Court's observations.
- The sequence is a model prelims case on the **constitutional hierarchy of rights, the duties of the police in public protest, and the circumstances under which criminal law discipline must yield to democratic participation**.
- *Source: Live Law; The Hindu; Indian Express, 27–30 Jul 2026*

### 1.3.7. SUPREME COURT URGES STANDALONE OFFENCE FOR 'DIGITAL ARRESTS'
- On **28 July 2026**, while hearing petitions on the menace of **'digital arrest' scams**, the Supreme Court **called for a separate, clearly defined offence and harsher punishment** for such frauds.
- Digital arrest scams involve fraudsters posing as police/CBI/customs officials over video calls, "arresting" victims online and coercing money transfers; losses of over ₹54,000 crore have been flagged to the Court.

## 2.1. INDIA–JAPAN 16TH ANNUAL SUMMIT (NEW DELHI, 1–3 JULY 2026)

**Why in News?**

- **Clean energy:** Cooperation on **hydrogen/ammonia co-methanation, biogas and sustainable aviation fuel (SAF)** was stepped up, complementing Japan's energy-transition needs with India's renewables push.

| Pillar | Outcome |

| Technology | AI/LLM, data centres, semiconductors, cybersecurity roadmap |
| Energy | Hydrogen/ammonia, biogas, SAF cooperation |
| Transport & infra | Mumbai–Ahmedabad HSR and allied infrastructure cooperation |

**Significance**
- Amidst US–China strategic competition, India and Japan — both members of the **Quad** and partners in the broader Indo-Pacific vision — are consolidating a **counter-dependency, rules-based alliance** in the region.
- The defence co-development agreement signals Japan's transition from a "pacifist" export-averse posture to a global defence-industrial player, and India's preference for tech partners beyond the Russia–China axis.

## 2.3. INDIA–CHINA: BORDER TRADE RESUMES, BUT BORDER TRUST REMAINS BRITTLE

**Why in News?**
- New Delhi and Beijing decided in **late July 2026** to **resume border trade through the Nathu La (Sikkim), Shipki La (Himachal Pradesh) and Lipulekh (Uttarakhand) passes from 1 August 2026** — ending the near-six-year suspension that followed the 2020 Galwan stand-off.
- External Affairs Minister **S. Jaishankar** and Chinese Foreign Minister **Wang Yi** met on the margins of the **ASEAN Post-Ministerial Conference in Manila on 22 July 2026**, with Jaishankar emphasising that **"peace in the border areas is key to the normalisation of India–China ties"**.
- Foreign Secretary **Vikram Misri** held talks with the **Chinese Vice Foreign Minister (26–27 July 2026)**, and satellite imagery released on **27 July** reported the forward deployment of **Chinese J-20 stealth fighters** at airbases close to the LAC.

**About the border file**
- India and China share a **~3,488 km LAC**; the 2020 stand-off at **Galwan, Pangong Tso and Depsang** left the status quo militarily contested, but a series of agreements in 2020–25 brought partial disengagement.
- **Border trade:** Nathu La (reopened 2006) and Shipki La, Lipulekh (1992) were channels for small-scale barter in local produce (from yak-tail wool and herbs to household goods); they were suspended after 2020 as part of border tensions. The July 2026 decision reopens all three.
- **Diplomatic architecture:** A layered dialogue — the **WMCC (Working Mechanism for Consultation & Coordination on India–China Border Affairs)**, Foreign Office Consultations (FOC), and the Special Representatives' (SR) mechanism — continues alongside summit-level contact (for instance at the Quad/ASEAN and BRICS forums).

| Milestone | Result |
|---|---|
| Galwan clash (Jun 2020) | 20 Indian soldiers killed; heightened LAC posture on both sides |
| Depsang disengagement (Oct 2024) | Two-phase pullback; patrol arrangements agreed |
| 45-point agreement (Apr 2025) | Confidence-building and de-escalation steps announced |
| Phase-2 disengagement (2025–26) | Draft post-departure verification; talks continue |
| Border trade reopening (announced Jul 2026) | Nathu La, Shipki La, Lipulekh to reopen from 1 Aug 2026 |

**Significance and challenges**
- **Cautious normalisation:** Reopening border trade restores local livelihoods and de-risks the relationship without conceding ground on territorial claims — a "confidence-building, not concession" approach.
- **Persistent asymmetry:** The reported **J-20 deployments** and infrastructure build-up on the Chinese side underline that military normalisation remains a work-in-progress; India's response includes forward airpower, the Indian Navy's IOR presence, and the **URGENT (usage of rivers/waters) coordination** forums.
- **For the exam:** remember the **July 2026 timeline** — Manila meet (22 Jul), FOC talks (26–27 Jul), border trade resumption decision (late Jul) — and the principle that **border peace is the stated precondition for broader ties.**

*Source: MEA; The Hindu; The Print; ANI, 22–28 Jul 2026*

## 5.1. CLOUDBURSTS IN INDIA: RISING HIMALAYAN HYDRO-HAZARDS

**Why in News?**
Repeated episodes of extreme monsoon downpours during July 2026 across Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh and Jammu & Kashmir triggered flash floods and landslides, once again turning the spotlight on cloudbursts as a distinct and under-studied hydro-meteorological hazard in the Himalayan region.

**About Cloudbursts**

- The India Meteorological Department (IMD) defines a **cloudburst** as an extreme, highly localised event in which an area of about **20–30 sq. km receives 100 mm (10 cm) or more of rainfall within one hour**.
- The volume of water released far exceeds the ground's capacity to absorb it, producing sudden and destructive flash floods, debris flows and landslides.
- Scientists also recognise a **"mini-cloudburst"** category (about 5 cm of rain in an hour over the same area), which can be equally devastating depending on local mountain topography.

**How a Cloudburst Occurs**

| Process | Description |
|---|---|
| Moisture incursion | Intense monsoon currents carry large quantities of moisture-laden air against steep slopes. |
| Orographic uplift | Mountains force this air to rise rapidly, causing sudden condensation. |
| Convective buildup | A single, deep cumulonimbus cloud stalls over a small area, releasing its entire water content in a short burst. |
| Cascade effect | Saturated slopes, deforested catchments and unplanned construction convert rainfall into violent surface runoff. |

**Why the Himalayas are Vulnerable**

- **Steep, fragile terrain** with thin soils and high gradients accelerates runoff and debris movement.
- **Changing climate** is increasing the frequency of short-duration, high-intensity rainfall events.
- **Land-use change** — deforestation, road-cutting, hydel projects and haphazard construction — reduces infiltration and slope stability.
- **Glacial retreat and permafrost thaw** add secondary hazards such as glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs).

**Impacts**

- Loss of life and livelihoods; destruction of roads, bridges and hydropower infrastructure.
- Displacement of hill communities; disruption of tourism and pilgrimage routes.
- Downstream flooding and sedimentation in the Gangetic plains.

**Response and Institutional Framework**

- IMD issues **nowcasts** and colour-coded warnings; Doppler weather radars cover parts of the Himalayan belt.
- **NDMA** guidelines on landslides, flash floods and GLOFs; State Disaster Management Authorities coordinate relief.
- Early-warning systems and community-based preparedness remain uneven across the region.

**Way Forward**

- Densify the **rain-gauge and radar network** and invest in high-resolution regional climate modelling.
- Enforce **land-use and construction regulations** in ecologically sensitive zones.
- Integrate cloudburst risk into **hydel, road and urban planning**; strengthen catchment treatment and afforestation.
- Build **community resilience** through mock drills, local early-warning dissemination and hazard mapping.

**Conclusion**
Cloudbursts are a potent illustration of how climate change interacts with fragile Himalayan geography. Disaster risk reduction must move from relief-centric responses to prevention, land-use discipline and robust early warning.

*Source: The Hindu, 31 July 2026; IMD; NDMA*

## 5.2. THE DRAFT CAFE-III NORMS AND INDIA'S ROAD TO CLEANER MOBILITY

**Why in News?**
The Ministry of Power, along with the Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE), released the **Draft Corporate Average Fuel Economy-III (CAFE-III) norms** for public and industry consultation, marking the next stage of India's regulatory push towards cleaner transport.

**About the CAFE Framework**

- CAFE norms mandate that an automaker meet a **sales-weighted average** fuel-efficiency and CO₂-emission target across its **entire annual fleet**, rather than for individual models.
- Administered under the **Energy Conservation Act**, they aim to cut greenhouse gas emissions, improve urban air quality and reduce crude oil imports.

| Phase | Timeline | Snapshot |
|---|---|---|
| CAFE-I | FY2017–18 | Baseline for fleet-efficiency tracking |
| CAFE-II | FY2022–23 | Tighter targets using an average vehicle-weight constant |
| CAFE-III | Draft July 2026; effective 1 April 2027 to FY2031–32 | Most ambitious standards yet |

**Key Features of CAFE-III**

- **Progressively tighter carbon targets:** fleet-average emissions decline from about **94.76 gCO₂/km (FY2027–28)** to a strict **~78.90 gCO₂/km by FY2031–32**.
- **Shift to the WLTP cycle:** migration from the laboratory-based Modified Indian Driving Cycle (MIDC) to the globally harmonised **Worldwide Harmonised Light Vehicles Test Procedure (WLTP)**, reflecting real-world driving.
- **Flexible compliance blocks:** compliance is assessed over a three-year block (FY28–30) and a two-year block (FY30–32), giving automakers room to phase in EV launches.
- **Carbon Neutrality Factors (CNFs):** first-ever mechanism to credit genuinely low-carbon technologies.
- **Credit buyout pricing:** firms facing a shortfall may purchase compliance credits from BEE starting at ₹2,500 per gCO₂/km (FY2028), rising to ₹4,500 by FY2032 — well below the Energy Conservation Act penalty threshold.

**India's Mobility Transition: The Wider Picture**

- The **IEA Global EV Outlook 2026** places EV share in India's new passenger-car sales at roughly **4% (2025)**, against nearly 55% in China, 27% in the EU and 10% in the US.
- Major domestic OEMs have **voluntarily pledged** an average 20–30% EV share by 2030.
- Support architecture includes the **FAME scheme**, **PM E-DRIVE**, and **Production-Linked Incentive (PLI)** schemes for advanced automotive technology.

**Why Clean Mobility Matters for India**

- **Energy security:** India imports the bulk of its crude, exposing the economy to West Asian supply shocks.
- **Air quality:** transport is a leading source of PM₂.₅ in major cities.
- **Industrial competitiveness:** strong domestic EV/hybrid supply chains prevent technological lock-out.
- **Climate commitments:** supports the Panchamrit targets and the net-zero-by-2070 goal.

**Challenges**
- Weak charging infrastructure and grid readiness; high upfront EV costs.
- Compliance burden and technology costs for smaller OEMs.
- Risk of credit-buyout becoming a cheaper substitute for genuine emission cuts.

**Way Forward**
- Link CAFE credit markets with a robust **carbon-compliance monitoring** regime.
- Accelerate charging networks and battery recycling under a circular-economy framework.
- Prioritise public transport electrification and freight efficiency alongside passenger cars.

**Conclusion**
CAFE-III signals a decisive shift from tokenism to measurable fleet decarbonisation. Its success will hinge on complementary infrastructure, credible enforcement and affordable clean technology.

*Source: Business Standard, 18 July 2026; The Hindu, 28 July 2026; BEE; IEA Global EV Outlook 2026*

## 6.1. MAHARASHTRA GIVES LEGAL RECOGNITION TO WOMEN FARMERS

**Why in News?**
Maharashtra became the **first Indian state to legally recognise women farmers** through the **Women Farmers Empowerment Act**, granting them access to welfare, credit and institutional support **without requiring land ownership**.

**About the Act**

- Historically, agricultural benefits, extension services and formal bank loans have been legally tied to **land ownership**, which is held overwhelmingly by men.
- The Act addresses this deep gender inequality in access to land, water, credit and natural resources.

**Key Data on Women in Agriculture**

- About **73% of the rural female workforce** in India is engaged in agriculture, compared to **47% of rural men**.
- Builds on Maharashtra's **Fourth Policy for Women (2024)** and aligns with the **National Policy for Farmers (2007)**.

**Core Legal Features and Institutional Mechanisms**

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| Broad definition of "farmer" | Includes women in crop husbandry, animal husbandry, poultry, fishery, agro-forestry, vermiculture, mushroom cultivation and primary processing — as owner **or** labourer. |
| Woman Farmer Certificate (WFC) | Official proof of occupation issued through the Gram Sabha or Nagar Panchayat, removing the land-title requirement for benefits. |

| Two-tier oversight | A State-Level Monitoring Committee (Chief Secretary) and a Governing Council (Chief Minister) for long-term policy direction. |

## 6.3. INDIA'S GEN Z AND UNEMPLOYMENT

**Why in News?**

**Who is Gen Z?**

**Key Data and Statistics**

- **Structural and social barriers:** lack of affordable childcare, safety and mobility constraints and unequal domestic expectations keep educated women out of paid work.

**Way Forward**
- Scale **apprenticeships, vocational training and industry-linked skilling**.
- Promote **labour-intensive manufacturing and services** to absorb young workers.

## 7.1. NISAR MISSION: PUBLIC RELEASE OF S-BAND DATA

**Why in News?**
ISRO began public release of S-Band radar data from the NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar (NISAR) mission through its Bhoonidhi Earth observation portal in late July 2026, marking a key transition to the mission's science phase.

**About NISAR**
- **Joint Mission:** NISAR is a collaborative mission between NASA and ISRO, launched on 30 July 2025 from Satish Dhawan Space Centre using GSLV-F16.
- **Dual-Frequency Radar:** It is the first spaceborne radar to carry both L-band and S-band frequencies on a single satellite, enabling all-weather, day-and-night imaging.
- **Wide Coverage:** Equipped with a 12-metre reflector antenna and SweepSAR technique, it captures swaths of approximately 240 km with high resolution.
- **Orbit & Revisit:** Orbiting at 747 km in a Sun-synchronous orbit, NISAR revisits the same location every 12 days.
- **Data Availability:** Starting with data from Cycle 25 (8 July 2026 onwards), S-band data is being made available on Bhoonidhi as it is processed. Older data will be reprocessed and added in phases.

**Significance**
- **Disaster Monitoring:** Enables tracking of floods, landslides, coastal erosion, and other rapidly changing events regardless of cloud cover or daylight.
- **Resource Management:** Supports precision agriculture, irrigation mapping, wetland monitoring, and coastal zone planning.
- **Cryosphere & Geophysics:** Facilitates monitoring of glacier movement, ice sheets, sea ice, and subtle surface shifts (subsidence).
- **Open Access:** Promotes broader scientific research and applications by making calibrated radar data publicly accessible.

**Way Forward**
- **Calibration Improvements:** Addressing known minor striping over featureless areas and occasional radio frequency interference through ongoing updates.
- **Expanded Applications:** Continued validation through ISRO's nationwide network of ground-based reflectors to enhance data accuracy for diverse user needs.

*Source: ISRO, Times of India, The New Indian Express, 27-28 Jul 2026*

## 8.1. Sarnath Inscribed as UNESCO World Heritage Site

**Why in News?**  
The ancient Buddhist site of Sarnath in Varanasi, Uttar Pradesh, was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List during the 48th session of the World Heritage Committee held in Busan, South Korea, in July 2026.

**About Sarnath**

- **Historical Significance:** Sarnath is the site where Lord Buddha delivered his first sermon, known as the "Turning of the Wheel of the Law" (Dharmachakra pravartana), after attaining enlightenment. This event led to the formation of the Buddhist Sangha.
- **Sacred Geography:** Known in Buddhist tradition as Rishipatana, Ishipatana, and Mrigadava.
- **Serial Property:** The inscribed property comprises two components—Chaukhandi Stupa and the Archaeological Remains of Sarnath (including Dhamekh Stupa, Dharmrajika Stupa, Mulagandhakuti Vihara, Ashokan Pillar, and associated remains).
- **Chronology:** Archaeological evidence indicates a stratified settlement spanning over 16 centuries, from the 5th–4th centuries BCE to the 12th century CE.
- **ASI Conservation:** Excavated and conserved extensively by the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI) for over 150 years; the ASI also manages the Sarnath Site Museum (established 1904), which houses key finds including the Mauryan Lion Pillar (now India's National Emblem) and the Gupta-period Dharmachakra-pravartana Buddha image.

**Outstanding Universal Value (UNESCO Criteria)**

- **Criterion (iii):** Bears witness to the enduring spiritual significance of Sarnath as one of the four principal destinations of Buddhist pilgrimage, demonstrated by the large number of religious buildings and monuments constructed and rebuilt over centuries.
- **Criterion (vi):** Directly associated with events of outstanding significance to Buddhism—Buddha's first sermon and the meeting of his first disciples.

**Significance**

- **Cultural Milestone:** Becomes India's 45th UNESCO World Heritage Site and Uttar Pradesh's fourth (joining the Taj Mahal, Agra Fort, and Fatehpur Sikri).
- **Long Journey:** Sarnath was included in UNESCO's Tentative List in July 1998 and formally inscribed in 2026 after a rigorous nomination process.
- **Global Recognition:** Reinforces India's commitment to preserving and showcasing its civilisational heritage on the world stage.
- **Tourism & Research:** Expected to strengthen the Buddhist Circuit and promote sustainable heritage tourism.

**Way Forward**

- **Conservation Focus:** Emphasis on sustainable tourism management, prevention of unplanned development in the buffer zone, and preservation of authenticity and integrity.
- **Community Engagement:** Continued collaboration with stakeholders to balance conservation with pilgrimage and visitor needs.

*Source: UNESCO, PIB, The Hindu, The Indian Express, 25-26 Jul 2026*
